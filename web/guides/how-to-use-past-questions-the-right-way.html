How to use past questions the right way

By Eduvora · Updated 12 October 2026

Past questions can be one of the best revision tools, or a trap that gives you false confidence. The difference lies in how you use them.

What past questions are good for

  • Showing the style of questions, such as how detailed answers need to be and how problems are worded.
  • Revealing priorities by showing which topics lecturers spend time on.
  • Training time management when you attempt them within the real time limit.
  • Exposing weak spots that reading alone never uncovers.

What they are not

A lecturer may change the questions each session, the course may be updated, and a topic that appeared often might be skipped next time. Past questions are a guide to style, not a promise of what will appear. Students who study only repeated questions often struggle when something new shows up.

Step 1: Learn the course first

Do not start with past questions on a topic you have never studied. Learn it from your notes and textbooks, then use questions to test what you learned. Otherwise you will memorise answers without understanding them.

Step 2: Attempt them honestly

Set a timer, close your notes and answer the whole paper. Write your answers down or choose them on screen before you look at anything. Peeking turns practice into reading, and reading is much weaker.

Step 3: Mark carefully and sort your mistakes

For each wrong answer, decide which of these caused it:

  1. You did not know the content.
  2. You knew it but misread the question.
  3. You made a calculation or recall slip.
  4. You ran out of time.

Each cause needs a different fix: study the content, slow down on reading, double-check arithmetic, or practise pacing. Also review questions you guessed correctly, because luck hides gaps.

Step 4: Understand the answer, not just the letter

Ask why the correct option is right and why each other option is wrong. If you cannot explain it in your own words, you have not learned it. Good explanations matter more than answer keys, and answer keys are sometimes wrong, so check them against your notes or textbooks and ask your lecturer if still unsure.

Step 5: Repeat after a gap

Return to the questions you missed after a few days. If you can now answer them without help, the learning has stuck. If not, revisit the topic itself.

Use questions responsibly

Only use papers that are legitimately shared. Do not use anyone's attempt to leak real exam questions, and do not help others cheat. Besides being dishonest, it can get you in serious trouble with your school.
